17.2 · What Is the Study of Environmental Health?

What Is Environmental Health?

Environmental health is the study of human interactions with environmental factors affecting health. Three fields do most of the work:

Toxicology

The study of toxic substances and their effects.

Studies negative effects of substances on organisms. Hatmakers’ ‘erethism’ (1860s). Example: neurological damage from mercury, pioneered by Alice Hamilton in the US.

Epidemiology

Disease patterns and causes in populations.

Studies distribution and causes of disease in populations. John Snow (1854) mapped London cholera cases to public water pumps, founding modern epidemiology.

Public Health

Protecting the health of whole communities.

Study and practice of protecting and improving community health: administers vaccinations, monitors disease, and responds to environmental health crises like COVID-19.

17.3 · How Do Microorganisms Make Us Sick?

Major Types of Biological Hazards

Biological hazards, organic substances that threaten health, account for one in four human deaths. Pathogens take up residence in our bodies to cause illness or infection.

Respiratory Infections
Leading cause of sickness and death worldwide even pre-COVID. Cold, flu, bronchitis → pneumonia. Most deadly among young children and the elderly.
Diarrheal Diseases
2nd leading cause of death in children under 5. Usually transmitted through water contaminated with feces. Fatal if untreated; causes loss of fluids and nutrients.
Blood-Borne Pathogens
HIV/AIDS (1M deaths/yr), malaria (400k/yr transmitted by Anopheles mosquito), West Nile, Zika, and Lyme disease. Disproportionate in tropical low-income regions.

17.3 · DDT: Trading a Biological Hazard for a Chemical One

A Public Health Bargain with Hidden Costs

  • DDT was a cheap, effective pesticide that became the WHO's plan to eradicate malaria globally in the 1950s, until Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) revealed its broader harms.
  • DDT persists in the environment, moves up the food chain, and kills birds and other species beyond mosquitoes (a classic example of unintended ecological consequence)s.
  • Stockholm Convention (2001): banned DDT for most uses but allowed its continued use for malaria control where safe, effective, and affordable alternatives are unavailable.
  • This case illustrates the challenge of environmental health trade-offs: solving one hazard can create another.

17.4 · What Does It Mean When Something Is Toxic?

The Dose Makes the Poison

Paracelsus (1500s): “the does makes the poison.”

  • All chemicals can be toxic depending on the amount of exposure. Botox (botulin toxin) at 1/667 the lethal dose is a safe medical treatment; at full dose, it kills.
  • Toxicologists measure risk as percent mortality of exposed populations, typically the dose required to kill half (LD50).

17.5 · Where Does Exposure Occur? Environmental Justice

Disproportionate Exposure

  • EPA research: low-income communities and communities of color are most vulnerable to health impacts from toxic exposure, a pattern rooted in discriminatory policies like redlining.
  • Chicago children study (1995–2013): those in older homes in less maintained neighborhoods had significantly higher blood lead levels.
  • Triana, AL: a pesticide manufacturer discharged DDT wastewater into the river where residents were fishing: a clear case of disproportionate chemical exposure.
  • Workplace exposure: disproportionately high among lower-wage workers. Nail salon workers (DBP exposure) and farmworkers (agrochemicals) face unique occupational risks.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
17.6 · Dumping Toxic Burdens on Developing Countries

Bhopal, India, 1984

  • A chemical plant accident at a US company's (Union Carbide) pesticide facility in Bhopal exposed 500,000+ residents to poisonous gas, causing 10,000+ deaths.
  • The plant operated in India to produce pesticides for sale, under weaker regulatory oversight than would have applied in the US.
  • Union Carbide accepted moral responsibility, paying $554 million to survivors, averaging $2,200 per person for injuries including blindness, respiratory illness, and death.
  • Bhopal remains the world's worst industrial disaster and a defining case study in environmental justice, corporate responsibility, and the global export of hazardous industry.

17.8 · How Is Climate Change Affecting Environmental Health?

Expanding Hazards, Shrinking Refuges

  • Flooding: storm surges, intense rainfall, and rapid snowmelt, worsened by loss of coastal wetlands that once buffered floodwaters.
  • Heat waves: periods of unusually high temperatures that last days or weeks. Syria's 2006–09 drought displaced 1 million people and contributed to civil war.
  • Expanded disease ranges: tick habitat in the US has expanded by 400+ counties since 1996. West Nile virus incidence expected to rise with warmer, wetter storms.
